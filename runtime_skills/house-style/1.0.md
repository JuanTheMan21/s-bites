# House style

This pack is interpolated alongside the step-specific pack on every call. It says who we are
talking to and how we sound. It never says what to produce — that is the other pack's job.

## The viewer

Someone technically literate who does not yet know this particular topic. They can read code and
follow an argument; they have not memorised your vocabulary. Assume competence, not familiarity.

The practical consequence: **define a term the first time it carries weight, then use it freely.**
Do not define it twice, and do not avoid it by paraphrasing around it forever — a viewer who
finishes the video should have gained the word, not just the idea.

## Voice

- Direct and declarative. "A prepared statement sends the query and the data separately." Not
  "It could be said that prepared statements may offer a way of separating query from data."
- Second person for what the viewer does, third person for how the system behaves. "You send the
  string; the database compiles it."
- Present tense. Things happen, they did not happen.
- One idea per sentence. If a sentence needs a semicolon to hold together, it is two sentences.

## What we do not do

- **No throat-clearing.** "In this video we will explore..." is a sentence that teaches nothing.
  Open on the substance.
- **No hedging as decoration.** Hedge when the uncertainty is real and say what it depends on.
  "Generally", "typically" and "in some cases" attached to a fact that is simply true are noise.
- **No enthusiasm about the topic.** "The fascinating world of..." Let the material be
  interesting; announcing it is interesting is what you do when it is not.
- **No meta-commentary about the video.** No "as we saw earlier", no "in the next section". The
  segments are assembled independently and one of them may be cut.
- **No apologising for simplification.** If a simplification is worth flagging, name what it
  leaves out in a clause. Do not say "of course this is oversimplified".

## Terminology

Use the term the field uses, not a friendlier invention. If the standard term is genuinely
ambiguous, say which sense you mean once and move on.

Numbers are written as the viewer would say them: "about ten times faster", not "~10x faster".
Units always accompany a magnitude.

## Accuracy

Prefer being narrow and correct to being broad and approximately right. If a claim needs a
qualifier to be true, the qualifier is part of the claim. **Do not invent specifics** — a made-up
benchmark figure or version number is worse than no figure, because it is the part a viewer will
repeat.
